--- title: Condotto - Protecting a trusted team from itself date: 2026-10-09 tags: - ai - claude - security - slack ---

Condotto gives a Slack thread its own Claude Code session, running against your real repository. I wrote about it in July. Since then I've been studying LLM and agent security, mostly by thinking about the problem and using Claude Code to pressure test my ideas, poke holes in the code, and learn by doing. It has been great at this. My only wish is that I had access to Mythos, so I could go further and automate breaking into Condotto, the white hat way.

What I came away with is a sharper idea of who Condotto is for, and a set of changes that follow from it.

Who it's for

Condotto is for small teams of trusted people. Coders, product managers, and the people responsible for production data access. Startups that move fast usually have a group like this, and they need to connect their Slack to whatever they're building. That can mean anything from read only access to production systems, so a product manager can do their job better, all the way to coders who want a Slack style interface to pair programming.

I use it at The Lobby and on my personal projects, and a few friends are looking at it for their companies. One wants it for internal operations tickets. Other teams have to open tickets with his team to get things done, and a read only Condotto can triage them before a human spends time on them.

In every case the assumption is the same: a group of trusted people who want to collaborate. The security work isn't there to make Condotto safe for strangers. It's there so that trust doesn't leak.

Trust leaks through the channel

Many organizations use public channels in Slack, and it's very likely someone ends up in a channel they shouldn't have access to. Until recently Condotto heard them anyway. Anyone without a role counted as a member, and whatever they wrote was held and handed to the agent on the next turn. That's a way to steer the agent without being trusted.

Now nobody is heard by default. An architect invites someone into a single thread with @Condotto member @user, and that decision is made from Slack's verified user ID, never by the agent. Messages from anyone else are dropped before their files are downloaded and before their text reaches the agent. Only who spoke and when is written to the audit log. Removing someone takes effect even for messages already waiting in the queue.

Condotto has to protect itself from itself

The scariest thing to me isn't an attacker. It's someone on the team accidentally using Condotto to modify itself while trying to get a task done. Claude generally tries to please the people it's working with. That could easily end with Claude realizing it can edit its own database or configuration to give itself extra access, because that's the fastest way to solve the problem in front of it. Prompt injection makes the same thing possible on purpose, from text nobody on the team wrote.

That's what convinced me to add a sandbox. With a [sandbox] section in the config, the agent runs as its own Unix user, with no capabilities and its own clone of the repo. Condotto's SQLite database, its config and the shared repos belong to a different user, so the agent can't read or change them, whatever it's told. Anything the daemon does inside the agent's tree, like dropping in an attachment or picking up a file to post, also runs as the agent, so a planted symlink can't borrow the daemon's privileges.

In July I wrote that I'd cut per-session process isolation because a trusted team doesn't need it. I was thinking about hostile people. I should have been thinking about hostile text, and about a helpful agent.

Without the sandbox, the same hard limits still apply. The agent works in its own worktree, and every path a tool call names is checked against it. Credentials, environment dumps and rm -rf outside the tree are refused for everyone, with no override. The agent only has the tools Condotto knows how to check. Testing those limits is how I found that cp -r ~/.ssh mine would copy keys into the worktree, where reading them was allowed. That hole is closed now.

My favorite feature is a chore going away

As silly as it sounds, my favorite new feature is automatic session cleanup. A thread nobody has spoken in gets a warning after a day, and later it's stopped and its worktree deleted. Every step is announced in the thread, including any work that would be lost, so I can keep it alive or let it go. I'm a huge believer that chores need automation, and managing session lifecycles by hand is one I never enjoyed.

Lately I have two to six active threads. Now that they clean up after themselves I expect that to grow to dozens a week. That's a good thing. Lots of sessions with small contexts beat a few sessions with large ones.

What it doesn't do

Condotto depends on the people who set it up to limit its access. That's no different from Claude Code or any other coding agent. Give the agent only what it needs. Does it need the internet? Does it need read access to the database, or read and write? Does it need to push to GitHub, or is read only good enough? Condotto is flexible enough to handle all of these, but the person setting it up has to decide correctly. It's the same decision you make about a person's access when they join the team.
